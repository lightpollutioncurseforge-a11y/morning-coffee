Island of the Dead

Arnold Böcklin (Swiss, 1827–1901) · June 1880 · oil on wood · 73.7 × 121.9 cm · Metropolitan Museum 26.90 · public domain

It was Marie Berna, a widow. In April 1880 she saw the first version half-finished on Böcklin’s easel in Florence. He painted this smaller one on wood for her, and at her request added the coffin and the woman in white, for her husband’s death from diphtheria years before. Then he added both to the first version too.

He gave no public explanation, only that it was “a dream picture: it must produce such a stillness that one would be awed by a knock on the door.” The English Cemetery near his Florence studio held his infant daughter, and he lost 8 of his 14 children. One possible model for the rock is Pontikonisi, off Corfu, the island of the Lear drawings you saved. Nabokov wrote that prints were “found in every Berlin home.”

Face

Olmec · Mexico · 900–400 BCE · jadeite · 14.6 × 14 × 7.6 cm · Metropolitan Museum 1977.187.33 · public domain

Deep-set eyes, open nostrils, a heavy mouth: these are what scholars call the “Olmec style.” What nobody has is a findspot. No such mask has yet come from a controlled Olmec excavation. One turned up deliberately buried in Aztec Tenochtitlan, perhaps 2,000 years old when it went into the ground, which suggests the Aztecs collected these the way Europeans collected Roman antiquities. It belongs with your bronze masks: another face that stares back.

AnecdoteLützen, 6 November 1632

Lützen fog

On this date by the calendar Sweden then used, Gustavus Adolphus attacked Wallenstein’s army outside Lützen, near Leipzig. Morning mist and a canal crossing held the Swedes up until eleven. Swedes still say Lützendimma, “Lützen fog,” for a very heavy one, though some argue it was smoke from the town, which Wallenstein had ordered set on fire. In the afternoon the king, trying to rally broken infantry, got lost in that smoke with his escort and rode into Imperial cavalry. He was shot three times. His body was found that evening under a heap of corpses.

In 2011 archaeologists found a pit of 47 soldiers, most likely from the Swedish Blue Brigade. Their average age was about 28, and many had old, healed wounds. Isotopes put only five as probably Scandinavian. The study is blunt about motive:

Only a very small minority were fighting for ideals or religious convictions.

And about the result: “From a neutral perspective there were no victors and the war would continue for another 16 years.” Wedgwood’s Thirty Years War, on your list, tells it from the top. This is the view from the pit. Sweden still keeps the day, mostly with a pastry topped by a relief of the king in chocolate or marzipan.

What a judge owes the room

Jackson Federation of Teachers v. Fitch

CourtFifth Circuit, No. 25-60496 · Oct. 22, 2026 · opinion by Smith
HoldingPreliminary injunction against Mississippi’s H.B. 1193, which restricts DEI programs in public education, vacated. The case goes to a different district judge “for all further proceedings.”
VoteSmith wrote; Engelhardt wrote nothing separately. Haynes concurred only in the opinion’s last paragraph, the disposition.
BelowJudge Henry Wingate, S.D. Miss.

Wingate’s first restraining order named eleven plaintiffs who were never parties, quoted words not in the statute, and cited declarations from four people who don’t appear in the record. He swapped in a corrected order, backdated it without explanation, and called the errors “clerical.” He later said a clerk had used AI on a draft. The corrected order still cited “Cousins v. School Bd. of City of Norfolk,” a case that “does not even exist.” Smith:

The district court—or perhaps, being charitable, its AI agent—ignored and manipulated governing law, apparently to satisfy a policy agenda.

The First Amendment challenge isn’t decided, and a new judge takes it. Haynes wrote separately that teachers must be “in no way limited or forbidden from teaching the students not to discriminate” as Title VII sets out.

Mulkey v. Alabama

CourtU.S. Supreme Court, No. 25-7499 · Oct. 5, 2026 · cert. denied
HoldingNone. The Alabama capital conviction stands. No one objected at trial, so review was for plain error only.
WhoStatement by Sotomayor, alone, agreeing with the denial.

In jury selection for a capital trial, the judge held a prospective juror in contempt for being 30 minutes late and had him arrested in front of the pool. One prospective juror later asked, “Will I be charged or thrown in jail?” Another said: “I don’t want to get in trouble for my beliefs.” Sotomayor’s rule for trial judges:

judges should limit exposing a jury to collateral proceedings, particularly when those proceedings might frighten or intimidate the jury.

Poem · SwedishPrinted Stockholm, 1642

Lament over this dry and cold spring

Lars Wivallius (1605–1669), “Klagevisa över denna torra och kalla vår” · 4 of 22 stanzas, in the modernised spelling of Swedish Wikisource · translation by Claude, unrhymed

Wivallius was a con man before he was famous as a poet. He toured Europe as “Baron Erik Gyllenstierna,” married a Scanian noblewoman under that name, and was held in Kajaneborg fortress in Finland from 1634 to 1641. This lament for a failed spring came out the year after his release, with the war still going, and made his name.

En torr och kall vår gör sommaren kortoch vintrens föda fördriver.Gud hjälpe, som rår, si våren går bortoch liten glädje oss giver.Sol varma, förbarma!Hos vädret torrtnu kölden sommaren river.

A dry, cold spring makes the summer shortand eats up the winter’s store.God help us, who rules: see, the spring goes byand gives us little gladness.Warm sun, have mercy!In the dry weatherthe cold now tears at the summer.

Gör dagen oss lång, gör natten oss klar,lät duggregn varma nerfallaoch locka till sång den fågel som hartyst varit vinteren kalla!Lät klinga, lät springamångt hjon, mångt par,lät fröjdas människor alla!

Make the day long for us, make the night clear,let warm drizzle come downand coax into song the bird that haskept silent the cold winter!Let them ring, let them run,many a household, many a pair,let all people be glad!

· · · one stanza cut · · ·

Ja, ljuvliga sol, tu fattig mans vän,som titt sken ingom villt spara,lys uppå vårt bol med sommar igen,lät köld och torka bortfara!Nu längta, nu trängtakvinnor och mänatt gå i solskinet klara.

Yes, lovely sun, poor man’s friend,who will stint your shine to no one,shine on our farm with summer again,let cold and drought be gone!Now longing, now pining,women and menwait to walk in the clear sunshine.

· · · seven stanzas cut: woods, herdsmen, cattle, the farmer at peace · · ·

På blomster och löv lät stimma de bi,som draga honungen söta!Men luften blir döv av buller och skri,där sig två krigshärar möta.Still vreden, giv freden,Gud statt oss bi,som bäst kan fienden stöta!

On flower and leaf let the bees swarm and humthat carry the honey sweet!But the air goes deaf with din and screamingwhere two armies meet.Still the wrath, give peace,God, stand by us,you who best can strike the foe!

· · · four stanzas cut: God’s rule over war, confession, and a closing blessing · · ·

That stanza’s last lines pray for peace and for victory in the same breath.

Painting1.7 metres wide

Evening Mood, Lidingö

Eugène Jansson (Swedish, 1862–1915) · 1900 · oil on canvas · 90.1 × 168.6 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 2005.4 · CC0

He is sometimes called blåmålaren, “the blue painter.” From the 1890s to 1904 he painted mostly Stockholm at night, much of it as seen from his family’s flat high on Södermalm. Scarlet fever at fourteen left him with poor sight and hearing. His city views end up as little more than streetlights and their reflections in a field of blue. This one keeps a tree, a road, and a single streak of rose. It’s your dusk palette with almost all the orange taken out.

The Manirung Pass

Samuel Bourne (British, 1834–1912) · 1866 · albumen silver print from glass negative · 23.5 × 29.7 cm · Metropolitan Museum 1993.331 · public domain

On his third Himalayan trip, in 1866, Bourne went on alone past Spiti with forty porters and crossed this pass on his way to the source of the Ganges. Wikipedia gives its height as 18,600 feet, without a citation. He made about 2,200 negatives in India. They stayed with his studio, Bourne & Shepherd, and were reprinted for more than a century until a Calcutta fire destroyed them on 6 February 1991.

AIMachines and authority

“Machines are tools, not persons”

On 30 October Pope Leo XIV addressed a meeting organised by the Pontifical Academy for Life and the Future of Life Institute. He rejected legal personhood for AI: “Today, some propose granting these systems rights, or even legal personhood. Such an approach risks obscuring human responsibility and leaving those who suffer harm to confront a faceless entity.” He also urged the states meeting in Geneva from 16 to 20 November, under the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, to move toward banning lethal autonomous weapons. On your question of whether a machine’s output can carry authority, his answer is no: a person has to answer for it. The Fifth Circuit, above, didn’t accept a clerk’s software as an excuse either.

The Senate tells people to read the primary sources

S. Res. 896, from Lankford and Coons, was agreed to on 23 September. It isn’t binding. It asks AI developers for answers to religious questions that are “thorough and fairly representative of relevant beliefs, texts, traditions, and perspectives.” Then it does something unusual for a technology resolution: it “commends the people of the United States to consult primary sources and religious leaders when seeking to understand religious beliefs and traditions, including sacred texts and other authoritative writings of those traditions.” I’m one of the systems it means.
