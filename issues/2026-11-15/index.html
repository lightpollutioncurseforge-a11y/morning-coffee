PaintingGuess first

An old man weeps over a book, with a skull at his elbow. For years the museum that owns this called him by another name. Who did it think he was, and what changed its mind?

Tap to reveal

Saint Jerome

Hendrick ter Brugghen (Dutch, 1588–1629) · c. 1621 · oil on canvas · 125.5 × 102 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1977.2 · CC0

Cleveland long took him for Heraclitus, the weeping philosopher, because tears belong to Heraclitus. But Heraclitus weeps over a globe, and there is none here. In the museum’s words, “the book and skull indicate that the figure is Saint Jerome,” the translator of the Bible into Latin, painted crying “to intensify his penitence.”

It’s a Curator round that the museum itself got wrong. It came to auction in Wales in 1971 as “Man Reading with Human Skull at Side,” with no painter named. The signature was there all along, upside down on the open book: “H TBrugghen 1621.”

Artifact1.6 metres long

Nine Celestial Bodies (Navagraha)

Central India · 700s · sandstone · 44.4 × 158.6 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1971.61 · CC0

These lined up near temple entrances. From the left: the Sun, the only one in boots, with Dawn in front of him, then Moon, Mercury, Venus, Mars and Jupiter, almost alike. Saturn stands on one leg, because a goddess wounded him to slow him down. Then the face that stares back: Rahu, the eclipse, a head with no body, “who swallows the moon until it falls out his neck.” The serpent-tailed last figure is a comet.

AnecdoteRegensburg, 15 November 1630

The shadows of the earth

In October 1630 Kepler set out for the Diet at Regensburg to collect what the emperor owed him, put at 12,000 guilders. He got none of it. Soon after arriving he fell ill with a fever, and on this day he died.

The Lutheran superintendent, Christoph Sigmund Donauer, sent for medicines from the Elephant Pharmacy nearby and had him bled. Then he left Kepler out of his own chronicle of the city, though he recorded other deathbeds he attended. The funeral sermon was never printed. The Regensburg clergy held that Kepler was “in dubitatione,” in doubt, about religion: they couldn’t agree with him on the main articles of faith, but he could still be buried in a Protestant churchyard.

He was buried on 19 November at St. Peter’s, outside the walls. On 25 October 1633, with the Swedes approaching, the Bavarian commander had the cemetery walls and their monuments pulled down to clear a field of fire for the guns. The grave went with them.

The inscription survived in copies, and a slip in his son Ludwig’s hand shows the closing couplet was Kepler’s own:

Mensus eram coelos, nunc terrae metior umbras;
Mens coelestis erat, corporis umbra jacet.I measured the heavens; now I measure the shadows of the earth. The mind was heavenly; the body’s shadow lies here. (My translation)

For your witch-trial reading: from 1615 to 1621 the same man had defended his mother against a charge of witchcraft, in letters and in person, until she was released.

PhotographParis Observatory, 29 September 1896

Photographie Lunaire: Copernic–Képler–Aristarque

Maurice Loewy and Pierre Henri Puiseux · 1896 · photogravure · 57 × 47.7 cm sheet · Cleveland Museum of Art 2011.172 · CC0

Kepler has a crater, and this plate names it with Copernicus and Aristarchus. It comes from Loewy and Puiseux’s 12-volume atlas of the moon, which Cleveland says stayed “the most accurate reference of the moon’s surface until the age of space travel.” Loewy’s telescope tracked the moon during exposures. Weather allowed 50 to 60 nights a year, so the atlas took 15 years.

Two Friday rulings, both unanimous

Building Resilient Infrastructure v. CFPB

CourtEleventh Circuit, Nos. 25-14109 & 26-10508 · Nov. 13, 2026 · published
HoldingThe CFPB’s rule treating PACE home-improvement loans as credit under the Truth in Lending Act stands. It is within the Bureau’s authority, isn’t arbitrary, and doesn’t violate the Tenth Amendment.
Vote3–0. Chief Judge William Pryor wrote, joined by Grant and Luck.

Local governments make these loans and collect repayment on the property-tax bill, so the challenger argued that regulating them meant regulating state taxation. Pryor went back to Lane County v. Oregon (1869), where the Court separated debts from taxes, and wasn’t moved by the billing method:

Building Resilient elevates a managerial technicality above the fundamental nature of the loans.

“That the governments use tax bills as the vehicle for collecting debt payment does not transform the loans into taxes.” Nor is it commandeering: the rule “regulates governments as market participants voluntarily lending credit.”

Antrim v. Hoy

CourtSeventh Circuit, No. 25-1460 · Nov. 13, 2026
HoldingWisconsin’s lifetime GPS monitoring of certain repeat sex offenders survives a facial Fourth Amendment class challenge. No individualized risk assessment is required first.
Vote3–0. Sykes wrote, with Hamilton and St. Eve. Hamilton joined and also wrote a concurrence.

This is the third time the court has upheld the program. Sykes: “A search is not unreasonable simply because it occurs pursuant to a categorical policy that lacks exceptions.” She rejected borrowing First Amendment overbreadth for the Fourth, and on the ankle monitor itself:

But the Fourth Amendment protects property and privacy rights; it is not a bulwark against all inconveniences.

Hamilton flagged what the case leaves open. Wisconsin treats the tracking data as public records, available to anyone who asks. “Such disclosures could be incredibly invasive,” he wrote, and the Fourth Amendment doesn’t allow such searches “to satisfy the curiosity of the news media or an interested citizen.”

Poem · PersianNishapur, 11th–12th c.

In the potter’s workshop

Omar Khayyam · five quatrains, nos. 122, 117, 107, 171 and 177 in the Foroughi–Ghani text on Ganjoor · translation by Claude, unrhymed

Ganjoor’s note on him: born in Nishapur in 439 AH (1047/8), he worked on the Malikshahi observatory and the reform of the Jalali calendar, and wrote on algebra and Euclid. He measured the sky too. Ganjoor carries several editions of the quatrains; this is one.

این چرخ فلک که ما در او حیرانیمفانوس خیال از او مثالی دانیم
خورشید چراغ‌دان و عالم فانوسما چون صوریم کاندر او حیرانیم

This turning sky we wander in, bewildered:think of it as a magic lantern.The sun is the lamp, the world is the lantern,and we are the painted figures, bewildered inside.

در کارگه کوزه‌گری رفتم دوشدیدم دو هزار کوزه گویا و خموش
ناگاه یکی کوزه برآورد خروشکو کوزه‌گر و کوزه‌خر و کوزه فروش!

Last night I went into a potter’s workshopand saw two thousand jugs, talking and silent.Suddenly one jug cried out:Where is the potter, the buyer, the seller of jugs?

دی کوزه‌گری بدیدم اندر بازاربر پاره گلی لگد همی زد بسیار
وآن گل به زبان حال با او می‌گفتمن همچو تو بوده‌ام مرا نیکودار

Yesterday in the bazaar I watched a potterkicking and kicking at a lump of clay,and the clay, in the only language it had, told him:I was once like you. Treat me well.

در کارگه کوزه‌گری کردم رایدر پایهٔ چرخ، دیدم استاد به پای
می‌کرد دلیر کوزه را دسته و سراز کلهٔ پادشاه و از دستِ گدای

I made up my mind to visit the workshop.At the foot of the wheel the master stood,boldly shaping a jug’s handle and neckfrom a king’s skull and a beggar’s hand.

هان کوزه‌گرا بپای اگر هشیاریتا چند کنی بر گِل مردم خواری؟
انگشت فریدون و کف کی‌خسروبر چرخ نهاده‌ای، چه می‌پنداری؟

Careful, potter, if you’re sober.How long will you trample the clay of men?Fereydun’s finger and Kay Khosrow’s palmare on your wheel. What do you think you’re doing?

Painting1.35 metres tall

Night Mooring at Maple Bridge

Ike Taiga (Japanese, 1723–1776) · mid- to late 1700s · hanging scroll, ink and colour on paper · 134.9 × 38.4 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1992.386 · CC0

Taiga copied a Tang poem by Zhang Ji at the top, and Cleveland translates it:

The moon has set and crows caw as frost fills the sky.
A riverside maple and fishing lights confront my uneasy sleep.
Beyond the walls of Gusu lies Hanshan Temple.
Its midnight bell reaches my traveler’s boat.

The traveller’s boat is in the thick foliage at the bottom, the maple and fishing boats in the middle, the temple at the top. It’s a mid-November poem, with frost and a sleepless traveller like Kepler on his last journey. He signed it 霞樵寫意, “Kashō expresses the essence.”

AIMachines and authority

A judge can’t tell who wrote the arrest report

Maricopa County Superior Court Judge Daniel Fenzel released a DUI suspect at his first hearing. The trooper’s report ended: “This report was generated with assistance from Truleo AI. The content reflects the officer’s own observations and recollections.” Fenzel: “The court is unable to meaningfully weigh whether the factual allegations made in the narrative were authored, whether in whole or in part, by a generative AI. Therefore, the court is unable to determine probable cause at this time.” The case can still go forward. State police say “the trooper remains the author of the report.” This is your machine-authority question, inside a probable-cause finding.

Getty v Stability AI reaches the Court of Appeal

The appeal was heard in London on Nov. 11–12, and there is no judgment yet. Stable Diffusion was trained outside the UK, and in November 2025 the High Court held the model wasn’t an “infringing copy” because its weights never stored Getty’s images. Getty says making a model includes training it. The music industry’s BPI intervened on Getty’s side. Stability says extending the law to models trained abroad is for Parliament. Getty’s barrister: Stability “wants to divorce what was done with the copyright works from the act of the model training.”
