Harvest on the Roman Campagna

John Gadsby Chapman (American, 1808–1889) · 1868 · oil on canvas · 35.5 × 91.4 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1915.688

Italy, painted by a Virginian for Americans. Chapman moved his family to Rome and, as Wikipedia puts it, made his living “selling paintings of rural Campania to American visitors.” Cleveland says he made about ten versions of this one. In the middle, the horses thresh the grain by trampling it.

He had painted America’s founding myths too. Congress paid him $10,000 for Baptism of Pocahontas, unveiled in the Capitol rotunda in 1840. The Civil War dried up the tourist trade, and he died a pauper in Brooklyn in 1889.

AnecdoteNew York, 26 November 1789

A day appointed by request

The first national Thanksgiving under the Constitution fell on this date, a Thursday, 237 years ago. On 24 September 1789 the House voted to send the amendments that became the Bill of Rights to the states. The next day Elias Boudinot of New Jersey moved that both houses ask the president for a day of thanksgiving. He said he “could not think of letting the session pass over” without one.

Two members objected. Aedanus Burke “did not like this mimicking of European customs, where they made a mere mockery of thanksgivings.” Thomas Tudor Tucker thought it wasn’t federal business: “If a day of thanksgiving must take place, let it be done by the authority of the States.” The resolution passed anyway. Washington’s proclamation, dated 3 October, begins by citing Congress’s request:

Now therefore I do recommend and assign Thursday the 26th day of November next to be devoted by the People of these States to the service of that great and glorious Being, who is the beneficent Author of all the good that was, that is, or that will beGeorge Washington, 3 October 1789

Among the things to give thanks for, Washington names “the peaceable and rational manner” in which constitutions were made. The petitions that follow are just as specific: to “pardon our national and other Transgressions,” and to make the government one of “wise, just, and constitutional laws, discreetly and faithfully executed and obeyed.” He asks for prosperity for all mankind, but only “such a degree” as “he alone knows to be best.”

Wikipedia, citing Mount Vernon, says he spent the day at St. Paul’s Chapel and sent beer and food to imprisoned debtors. I couldn’t check that against his diary.

Turkey Vessel

Costa Rica, southern Nicoya region, Pataky Polychrome style · c. 1000–1550 · ceramic, slip · 26.3 × 20.3 × 18.4 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1945.376

The bird on today’s table was American long before the table. According to Wikipedia, today’s domestic turkey descends from a wild subspecies of central Mexico. Ancient Mesoamericans domesticated it at least 2,000 years ago, for meat, eggs and feathers, and the Spanish took it to Europe. Cleveland gives the vessel no commentary, only its name, place and date, so I won’t guess what it was for. The orange on cream suits your rust-and-vermilion streak.

Publius’s donors, and the law a court is supposed to know

The Buckeye Institute v. IRS

CourtSixth Circuit, No. 25-3170 · Nov. 25, 2026 · recommended for publication
HoldingCongress may require § 501(c)(3) charities to report their substantial contributors to the IRS as a condition of the double tax benefit (exemption plus deductible gifts). That is a condition on a subsidy, not a compelled disclosure subject to exacting scrutiny under AFP v. Bonta. Reversed, with judgment for the IRS.
Vote2–1. Griffin wrote, joined by Cole. Readler dissented.

Buckeye, an Ohio think tank, must list on Schedule B donors who give more than $5,000 in a year, if that is more than 2% of its contributions. It sued after the Supreme Court struck down California’s demand for the same list in 2021. The majority says the federal rule stays inside the tax program, and points out that a (c)(4) affiliate can take anonymous money:

Put simply, the reporting requirement does not reach beyond the contours of the federal Tax Code and thus does not violate the Constitution either.Griffin, J.

Readler went back to the founding:

Look no further than the Federalist Papers, published pseudonymously under “Publius,” to recognize the value anonymous speech has played in American expression since the founding.Readler, J., dissenting

Donors to a (c)(3), he wrote, give “with the knowledge that the federal government is watching,” and courts must check the government’s account of its own program, “lest we leave the fox in charge of the hen house.”

Baxter v. Cheadle

CourtSeventh Circuit, No. 26-1199 · Nov. 24, 2026 · published
HoldingA plaintiff doesn’t lose qualified immunity fights for failing to cite the best cases. The court applies the law it knows, and a for-cause employee’s right to a hearing “has been clearly established for 54 years.” Whether this officer was a for-cause employee is a question of fact. Vacated and remanded.
Vote3–0. Easterbrook wrote for Pryor and Taibleson.

The Village of Palestine, Illinois, population 1,233, fired a part-time police officer, who said he’d been given no chance for a hearing. The district judge granted the chief immunity because the officer’s lawyer hadn’t cited the controlling cases: “courts do not jump in to help a party (especially one represented by counsel) when the party falters.” Easterbrook:

This mandatory-citation approach misunderstands the judicial role.Easterbrook, J.

Parties must raise their claims, he says, but the court supplies the law. Everything then turns on whether the village ever adopted a state civil-service scheme by referendum. The panel searched “standard legal sources and the Internet” and found no sign that it had: “Small villages (and even some federal agencies, before the Administrative Procedure Act) have been known to put rules in filing cabinets.”

Poem · GermanAsmus, 1783

Wir pflügen und wir streuen

Matthias Claudius (1740–1815) · three of five stanzas, with the refrain, in the hymnal form printed on German Wikipedia · stanzas 4–5 cut · translation by Claude, unrhymed

You know this as “We Plough the Fields and Scatter.” It began as the peasants’ song in Paul Erdmanns Fest, Claudius’s sketch of a harvest festival on an estate, where a lead singer and “all the peasants” alternate. German Wikipedia says the piece sets the farmhands’ human nobility against their slightly arrogant lords. Jane Montgomery Campbell’s 1861 English version kept the focus but didn’t translate strictly. Here is a plainer English.

Am Anfang war’s auf Erden noch finster, wüst und leer;und sollt was sein und werden, mußt es woanders her.So ist es zugegangen im Anfang, als Gott sprach;und wie es angefangen, so geht’s noch diesen Tag.

In the beginning the earth was still dark and waste and void;if anything was to be and grow, it had to come from elsewhere.That is how it went in the beginning, when God spoke,and as it began, so it goes to this day.

Alle gute Gabe kommt her von Gott dem Herrn,drum dankt ihm dankt, drum dankt ihm danktund hofft auf ihn.

Every good gift comes from God the Lord,so thank him, thank him, thank him, thank him,and hope in him.

Wir pflügen, und wir streuen den Samen auf das Land,doch Wachstum und Gedeihen steht in des Himmels Hand:der tut mit leisem Wehen sich mild und heimlich aufund träuft, wenn heim wir gehen, Wuchs und Gedeihen drauf

We plough, and we scatter the seed on the land,but growing and thriving are in heaven’s hand:with a soft breath it opens, mildly and in secret,and when we go home it drips growth and thriving on it.

Er sendet Tau und Regen und Sonn und Mondenscheinund wickelt seinen Segen gar zart und künstlich einund bringt ihn dann behende in unser Feld und Brotes geht durch unsre Hände, kommt aber her von Gott.

He sends dew and rain and sun and moonlightand wraps his blessing up, so delicately and with such art,and brings it quickly into our field and bread:it passes through our hands, but it comes from God.

[Refrain after each stanza. Stanzas 4–5 cut.]

SongThe grain’s side of the story

Traffic, “John Barleycorn (Must Die)”

John Barleycorn Must Die · Island, 1970 · recorded February–April 1970 at Island and Olympic, London · traditional, arranged by Steve Winwood

Claudius thanks heaven for the grain. This ballad gives the grain’s side. Barley is personified and suffers attacks and a death that follow the stages of its cultivation, reaping and malting, before it comes back as beer. It’s Roud 164, its written forms go back to the sixteenth century, and Burns made his own version in 1782. Wikipedia places Traffic’s reading beside Pentangle and Fairport Convention, which puts it near Steeleye Span in your likes. Winwood sings and plays acoustic guitar and piano.

PaintingNew England, 1912

The Apple Gatherers

Karl Anderson (American, 1874–1956) · 1912 · oil on canvas · 134 × 127.6 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1926.485

The comfortable end of the harvest. Cleveland says Anderson disliked the industrial cities of his day and painted country scenes “seemingly untouched by modern development.” He said his pictures were meant to catch “the fragrance of the beauty of life.” The apples are McIntoshes, which Cleveland says make up two-thirds of New England’s apple harvest today. It’s sincere and unironic, like the jewel-toned gardens you saved.

PhotographNorfolk Broads, 1886

Rowing Home the Schoof-Stuff

Peter Henry Emerson (British, 1856–1936) · 1886 · platinum print, plate XXI of Life and Landscape on the Norfolk Broads · 13.6 × 27.6 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1983.206

A harvest you can’t eat. Cleveland says a “schoof” is a sheaf of marsh plants, cut to be dried and stored at home. Emerson set out to photograph country life directly. He attacked the fashion for combining several negatives into one picture, the technique Le Gray used for yesterday’s seascape, and called it false. One small figure, a lot of water, and the last light.

Artifact1.2 cm across

Cunobeline Quarter Stater: Corn and Horse

England · c. 10–40 CE · gold · 1.2 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1969.153

An ear of grain struck on a coin by a British king. Cleveland says the ear “is frequently found on coins of Cunobeline.” It is unkind about the horse on the back: the head and neck are badly proportioned, and the animal “has acquired an additional joint in its right foreleg.” He ruled much of south-east Britain from about AD 9 to 40. Suetonius calls him Britannorum rex, and Strabo lists grain among Britain’s exports to Rome. Through Geoffrey of Monmouth and Holinshed he became Shakespeare’s Cymbeline, in name only.

AIMachines and authority

Ohio suspends a lawyer who wouldn’t admit her cases weren’t real

On 24 November the Ohio Supreme Court unanimously and indefinitely suspended a Cleveland lawyer, Arleesha Wilson, on a five-count complaint that included neglecting several clients’ matters and making false statements to two tribunals. In one federal case she cited four cases that don’t exist. Ordered to produce them, she sent 1,395 pages of case law that didn’t include them. When the judge asked whether she had used AI, she said she had. Asked whether she would certify that the four cases existed, she “answered, ‘No,’ before acknowledging that she was unable to find them.” She also hasn’t made “even one symbolic payment” toward more than $647,000 in restitution.

A victim statement written to make the judge weep

The Guardian and the Bureau of Investigative Journalism counted at least 25 cases in England and Wales this year where AI caused problems, judging from published judgments. In one, a police officer uploaded a mother’s interviews to Microsoft’s Copilot and asked it “to achieve the highest sentence and the most tear-jerking VPS to make a judge or reader weep when reading.” The Court of Appeal warned that “no police officer should ever prepare a witness statement of any kind in this way.” I read AOL’s syndicated copy, not the judgments.

“A.I. cannot create God,” and the members are drifting

Researchers at BYU-Idaho and USC surveyed 1,797 U.S. Latter-day Saints in 2025 and 2026, to see whether leaders’ warnings were getting through. Gerrit W. Gong had said in August 2025: “As a creation of God, man can create A.I., but A.I. cannot create God.” The share who think it’s “appropriate” to use AI for advice on personal ethical dilemmas rose from about a quarter to 42%.
