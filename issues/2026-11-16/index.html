A man in fur and velvet half-rises from a table. One hand stays on a sheet of paper; the other is flung toward something we can’t see. What’s on the paper, and what is he pointing at?

Portrait of a Man, Possibly Girolamo Rosati

Lorenzo Lotto (Italian, 1480–1556) · 1533–34 · oil on canvas · 108.2 × 100.5 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1950.250 · CC0

Nobody knows, and the museum says so: “The gesture of the elegantly dressed man is a mystery.” Two readings. The clover, jasmine and roses suggest a marriage portrait, maybe with a lost companion picture of his bride. Or he is Girolamo Rosati, a high official in Fermo, and the paper “may be a design for one of his important architectural projects, to which he may be pointing in the distance.”

Even the date is partly missing. The signature reads “.L. Lotus 15[...]”. Cleveland dates it 1533–34. In 1533 Atahualpa, whose capture is below, was executed.

AnecdoteCajamarca, 16 November 1532

Five or six paces

Pizarro’s company of about 180 reached Cajamarca on Friday the 15th and hid in the buildings around the empty square. On Saturday afternoon Atahualpa came in on a litter lined with macaw feathers, his guard “dressed in a livery of different colours, like a chess board,” sweeping the road ahead of him. Pizarro’s secretary, Francisco de Xerez, wrote down what the Dominican Vicente de Valverde said through an interpreter:

I am a Priest of God, and I teach Christians the things of God, and in like manner I come to teach you. What I teach is that which God says to us in this Book.Xerez, in Markham’s translation (1872)

Atahualpa asked to see it. When Valverde reached to open it, he struck his arm away and opened it himself, “without any astonishment at the letters and paper,” and threw it five or six paces. Then he answered: “I know well how you have behaved on the road, how you have treated my Chiefs, and taken the cloth from my storehouses.”

Valverde went back and reported that the book had been thrown to the ground. Pizarro went in with four men, seized Atahualpa by the arm and cried “Santiago,” and the guns fired. Xerez himself writes, “During the whole time no Indian raised his arms against a Spaniard.” Many scholars now call it the Cajamarca massacre.

The only Inca account, dictated decades later by Titu Cusi Yupanqui, son of Atahualpa’s half-brother Manco, tells it differently. At a meeting days earlier, Atahualpa offered two Spaniards a golden cup of chicha. “The Spaniard poured it out.” They gave him a letter or book they called quillca, writing, of God and their king, and he threw the “letter or whatever it was” on the ground. The grievance about cloth fits the rest of today’s issue: his empire counted its storehouses with knots.

Artifact1.37 metres across

Inka khipu

Peru, Inca period · c. 1400–1532 · cords, knotted and twisted; cotton and wool · 113 × 137.2 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1940.469 · CC0

A year after Cajamarca, Pizarro’s brother Hernando wrote to the royal court at Santo Domingo, partly because others were giving “inconsistent accounts.” In the Inca towns, he reported:

They count by certain knots on cords, and so record what each chief has brought. When they had to bring us loads of fuel, maize, chicha, or meat, they took off knots or made knots on some other part; so that those who have charge of the stores keep an exact account.

At Jauja, Hernando was told, Atahualpa’s captain-general had “counted them by his knots in presence of the Christians”: thirty-five thousand men. For your art-of-memory shelf, this is a memory you can hold in your hands. Cleveland’s record says nothing more about what this one counts, and I couldn’t get to other sources today.

The benchRead from the opinions

A registry, and a uniform

Doe #1 v. Lee

CourtSixth Circuit, No. 25-6122 · Nov. 13, 2026 · published
HoldingTennessee’s “violent sexual offender” and “offender against children” labels apply retroactively to eight men whose offenses predate the registry, but they aren’t punishment, so there’s no Ex Post Facto violation. Their challenge to where they may live and work fails for standing against the state’s bureau director. The injunction is vacated.
Vote3–0. Siler wrote, with Nalbandian and Davis. No separate opinions.

The lesson is in keeping two questions apart. Applying a new label to old conduct, Siler writes, “does not merely draw on an antecedent fact; it changes the legal consequences of completed conduct.” So the law is retrospective. But it can be retrospective without punishing:

The classifications operate retrospectively, but they do not impose punishment.

On standing, the director could add a note to each man’s profile. “That step may be feasible. But feasibility is not redressability.”

Dickinson v. Trump

CourtNinth Circuit, No. 26-1609 · Nov. 10, 2026 · published
HoldingThe preliminary injunction limiting federal crowd-control force outside Portland’s ICE office, and the provisional class certification, are vacated. The plaintiffs aren’t likely to win their First Amendment retaliation claim, and the injunction was overbroad.
Vote2–1 in part. Lee wrote; Tung was the third judge. De Alba concurred in part and dissented in part.

Thursday’s issue carried another Ninth Circuit panel on Portland tear gas, so this is the same street from a second panel. Lee opens: “But the First Amendment cannot be a sword to justify vandalism of federal buildings, criminal trespass of government property, or obstruction of law enforcement.” The district court had also ordered officers’ uniforms redesigned with conspicuous markings. Lee:

Courts cannot be the couture of the executive branch.

De Alba agreed that three provisions went too far, the uniform one included. She would have kept the ban on firing munitions “at the head, neck, or torso of any person, unless the officer is legally justified in using deadly force against that person,” and the class. This speech, she wrote, “rest[s] on the highest rung of the hierarchy of First Amendment values.”

Poem · QuechuaPeru, date unknown

Apu Inka Atawallpaman

“To the Lord Inca Atawallpa” · anonymous · excerpts, in the Quechua of Odi Gonzales’s edition (Pakarina, 2014; reissued 2024) · translation by Claude, made with Gonzales’s Spanish beside it

The elegy for Atahualpa was unknown in print until a harpist from Cuzco gave a manuscript copy to José M. Benigno Farfán, who published it in 1942. Arguedas heard Jorge Manrique’s Spanish elegy for his father in it. Gonzales calls it “the most accomplished Quechua text of Peruvian poetry in five centuries.” The poem has him beheaded; in Wikipedia’s account he was garroted in 1533.

Ima k’uychin kay yana k’uychiSayarimun?Qosqoq awqanpaq millay wach’iIllarimun,Tukuy imapi saqra chikchiT’akakamun

What rainbow is this, this black rainbowrising?A hideous light has dawnedagainst Cuzco;an evil hailscatters on everything.

Watupakurqan sunqollaymiSapa kutin,Musqoyniypipas ch’eqmi ch’eqmiUti uti,Chiririnka qhenchataraqmi,Aqoy phuti.

My heart foresaw it,time and again,and in my dreams, startled,stunned,I saw the blowfly of ill omen:doom and grief.

Inti tutayan q’elluyaspan,Huk watuypi;Atawallpa ayachaspaChay sutinpi,Wañuynillanta chikachaspaHuk ch’illmiypi.

In one omen the sun yellowsand goes dark,shrouding Atawallpa’s body,putting out his name,his line,in the blink of an eye.

Umallantas wit’unkuñaMillay awqa;Yawar mayus purisqanñaP’alqa p’alqa.

They say they have cut off his head,the hideous enemy.They say a river of blood is running,forking into branches.

· · · his teeth, his eyes; then all nature mourns · · ·

Qaqapas ch’ilan apunmantaWankhakuspan;Mayupas qaparin phutiymantaHunt’akuspan.

The hard rock gives way for its lord,hollowing itself;the river roars with griefas it floods.

· · · the people, scattered, plead with the dead Inca · · ·

Ñukñu wach’iq ñawillaykitaKicharimuy,Ancha qokoq makillaykitaMast’arimuyChay samiwan kallpanchasqataRipuy niway

Open your eyesthat shine so clear;stretch out to usyour generous hands,and, strong with that good sign,tell us: Go back.

PaintingA circle, 54.5 cm

Scenes of Witchcraft: Evening

Salvator Rosa (Italian, 1615–1673) · c. 1645–49 · oil on canvas · Cleveland Museum of Art 1977.37.3 · CC0

For your witch-trial shelf. At dusk the witches crowd a cauldron. A conjured skeleton holds an hourglass, and in its other hand a capital letter A with a plumb line hanging from it. Cleveland reads these as “ancient signs of the moon and judgment.” Rosa also wrote a poem, “La Strega,” whose witch lists “ground powders, mystic gems, snakes and owls, stinking blood. . . .” The round canvas is deliberate: the museum says Rosa chose it for “the foundational role of the circle in practicing magic.”

SongLima chicha

Los Hijos del Sol, “Cariñito”

Written by Ángel Aníbal Rosado (1939–2008) · on Barbès Records’ compilation The Roots of Chicha (2007)

Chicha was the corn drink the Spaniard poured out at Cajamarca. In Lima, by NPR’s account, it became the name for Peruvian cumbia with electric guitar. Rosado was born in Cochahuayco, near Huarochirí, and wrote this for his wife. It has been covered across the Americas and Europe. The date isn’t settled: Peru’s Panamericana says it came out in 1978, while a Substack history of chicha calls it a 1969 hit. I haven’t heard it.

Autonomous weapons: the CCW review opens today

The states parties to the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons open their review conference in Geneva today, and it runs to Friday. Our Nov. 8 issue flagged it. The chair-designate’s draft aims at a draft protocol by 2028. A US working paper rejects a protocol. In Tech Times’s summary, it argues that autonomy is a capability inside many systems, not a type of weapon. Decisions need consensus. From Amnesty’s Carolyn Horn: “Autonomous weapons systems are no longer a hypothetical future threat, with reports growing that they are already being tested, integrated, and deployed.”

Delhi High Court: a judge steps in where there’s no statute

Justice Manoj Jain ordered the takedown of AI-generated videos and posts, published from the Aam Aadmi Party’s official accounts, that showed Delhi’s chief minister, Rekha Gupta, as a liquor vendor. By SCC Online’s account, he reasoned that “in the absence of appropriate stringent legislative regulation of such content, it becomes the judicial duty to examine whether the use of AI has crossed the threshold.” The line he drew: “Healthy criticism and satirical humour may not raise any eyebrows but AI-generated videos and the posts in question have clearly breached the dividing line.” The case returns on Dec. 4.

Philippine bishops hang banners against “Pax Silica”

Ahead of President Trump’s visit for the ASEAN summit, Bishop Jose Elmer Mangalinao of Bayombong asked parishes, schools and town halls to hang banners today against large-scale mining and Pax Silica. That is the US-led supply-chain initiative for chips, AI infrastructure and critical minerals, which he warned could intensify extraction for military technology and AI. The prelates’ statement: “No promise of economic development can justify violations of human dignity, the disregard of Peoples’ rights, or the destruction of the natural resources upon which communities depend.” It’s the theology of technology, starting from the mine.
