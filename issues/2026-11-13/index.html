The Race Track (Death on a Pale Horse)

Albert Pinkham Ryder (American, 1847–1917) · c. 1896–1908 · oil on canvas · 70.5 × 90 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1928.8 · CC0

Cleveland says the subject came from a New York horse race in 1888. A friend of Ryder’s bet $500 on it, and when the horse lost he died by suicide. Ryder answered with medieval symbols: Death as a skeleton cutting down the living with a scythe, and a snake for temptation. He worked on the canvas for years and “was deeply reluctant to part with it.”

That habit had a cost. Ryder mixed in “candle wax, bitumen, and non-drying oils,” and his paintings “remain unstable and become much darker over time.” Some “are still soft even a century later.”

It belongs with the dread in your saved images. It also uses your small-figure-in-a-big-landscape device, except that here the small figure is the one doing the harm.

Song · 3:10Algiers, for the screen

Ahmed Malek, “Omar Gatlatou”

Musique Originale de Films · Habibi Funk 003 · 2016 · track 1, a longer version that was never released before · from Merzak Allouache’s film Omar Gatlato

Malek conducted the Algerian Television Orchestra for decades and scored dozens of films, TV shows and documentaries. Sources disagree on whether he was born in 1931 or 1932, and he died in Algiers in 2008. In 2016 Habibi Funk reissued the music, after reaching his family through a chain of acquaintances: “Her Algerian friend was the neighbor of Ahmed Malek’s daughter!”

In the film, a friend gives the hero a tape, and he falls for the voice of a woman on it, Selma. English Wikipedia credits the film’s music to two people, so who scored which scene isn’t clear. The label’s head, Jannis Stürtz, describes the mood as “melancholic and reflective, emotional and touching, but never depressing.” It’s a rediscovery like Cambodian Rocks, and a soundtrack, which is how a lot of your likes arrive.

Artifact40 cm

Stilt footrest

Marquesas Islands, Polynesia · early 1800s · wood · 40 × 7.7 × 11.8 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1970.114 · CC0

A figure crouches under the step, with one white eye looking out. Cleveland says stilt walking and “knockdown stilt contests” were favourite amusements in the Marquesas, and champions took on other tribes at great festivals. This rest was lashed to a six-foot pole with sennit cord run through the slit at the figure’s back. Specialist carvers cut them from hardwood and cured them in the mud of a taro patch.

Small enough to hold, with a face that looks back, and from the Pacific, which your saved objects don’t reach yet.

AnecdoteBorn 13 November 354

Then I slept

Augustine was born on this day in 354 at Thagaste, now Souk Ahras in Algeria. In 387 his mother, Monica, died at Ostia, Italy, as they prepared to sail home to Africa. In Book IX of the Confessions he tells what he did with his grief, which was mostly to refuse it.

When she died, his son Adeodatus “burst out into wailing” and was hushed. Augustine got through the psalm and the burial without weeping, and the visitors “thought me to be devoid of any sense of sorrow.” Then he tried a bath, having heard that the word came from Greek “because it drives trouble from the mind”:

I bathed, and felt the same as before I had done so.Confessions IX.12, Pilkington’s translation

“Then I slept, and on awaking found my grief not a little mitigated.” Lying alone, he remembered the evening hymn of Ambrose, the bishop who baptized him. Only then did he let himself weep, “during so small a part of an hour,” and he warns any reader who would call it a sin: “let him not laugh at me.” Soon after they reached Africa, Wikipedia adds, Adeodatus died too.

Poem · LatinMilan, 4th c.

Deus creator omnium

Ambrose of Milan · evening hymn · complete, 8 stanzas · Latin text from The Latin Library · translation by Claude, unrhymed

This is the hymn Augustine remembered. When he quotes it, the first stanza reads decora and sopora; the Latin Library text printed here has decoro and soporis, with the same sense. The line to keep is the eighth, the one that met his grief.

Deus creator omnium,Polique rector, vestiensDiem decoro lumine,Noctem soporis gratia.

God, maker of all things,ruler of the sky, who clothethe day in lovely light,the night in the gift of sleep,

Artus solutos ut quiesReddat laboris usui,Mentesque fessas allevet,Luctusque solvat anxios.

so that rest may give backour loosened limbs to their work,lift up tired minds,and loosen anxious griefs.

Grates peracto jam die,Et noctis exortu preces,Votis, reos ut adjuves,Hymnum canentes solvimus.

Now the day is done, our thanks,and as night rises, our prayers,we pay as vows, singing a hymn,that you may help the guilty.

Te cordis ima concinant,Te vox canora concrepet,Te diligat castus amor,Te mens adoret sobria.

Let the heart’s depths sing you,the ringing voice sound you out,chaste love hold you dear,the sober mind adore you,

Ut cum profunda clauseritDiem caligo noctium,Fides tenebras nesciat,Et nox fide reluceat.

so that when the deep dark of the nightshas shut the day away,faith may know no darknessand night shine back with faith.

Dormire mentem ne sinas,Dormire culpa noverit,Custos fides refrigerans,Somni vaporem temperet.

Do not let the mind fall asleep;let guilt learn how to sleep.May faith, a cooling guard,temper the vapour of sleep.

Exuta sensu lubrico,Te cordis alta somnient;Nec hostis invidi doloPavor quietos suscitet.

Stripped of slippery sense,let the heart’s heights dream of you,and let no fear, by the envious enemy’s guile,startle us from our rest.

Christum rogemus et Patrem,Christi Patrisque Spiritum,Unum potens per omniaFove precantes Trinitas.

Let us ask Christ and the Father,and the Spirit of Christ and the Father:one God, mighty through all things,Trinity, cherish us who pray.

A principal’s letter and a millstone verse

Boone v. Lowell Area Schools

CourtSixth Circuit, No. 25-1948 · decided Nov. 10, 2026 · recommended for publication
HoldingReversed in part. A parent plausibly pleaded that a principal’s letter telling her to stop naming district employees online was a prior restraint. Dismissal of her challenge to a no-contact directive, and of her other federal claims, affirmed.
PanelJudges Davis, Mathis and Ritz. Davis wrote; Ritz concurred in part and dissented in part.

Stefanie Boone, a Michigan mother of three, spent two and a half years on a Facebook page attacking the district’s DEI training and library books, often by naming staff. In early 2024 she linked an article about a teacher and quoted Matthew 18:6, the millstone verse. The principal wrote that the post could “be construed as threatening in nature.” He asked her to “immediately cease and desist from any reference to any Lowell Area Schools employee” online, and warned of “all available legal remedies.”

The majority reads that as coercion. Bantam Books involved threatened prosecution, but

the prospect of a serious civil suit is equally likely to stifle a would-be speaker. Even defense of a frivolous claim can run up quite the bill.

The no-contact rule survives: “Boone has no First Amendment right to quibble with the District employees of her choice.” This is only the pleading stage. Ritz reads the letter in context as “a formal and firm ‘request’” after a post suggesting a teacher be “drowned in the depth of the sea.” He adds: “A high school principal is more lightly armed, in both a literal and figurative sense, than a sheriff or a corrections officer.”

Artifact14 cm without its base

Sleep and Death cista handle

Etruscan · 400–375 BCE · bronze · 14 × 17.4 cm · Cleveland Museum of Art 1945.13 · CC0

This was the handle on the lid of a cista, a large cylindrical bronze box. Two winged figures carry a dead man between them. Cleveland identifies them as probably Sleep and Death with Sarpedon, the son of Zeus, who was killed by Patroclus in Iliad XVI. Apollo took his body from the battle and gave it to the pair. The museum allows another candidate, Memnon, killed by Achilles. Look at the bearers’ heads: Sleep and Death wear crested helmets, like soldiers. The group was cast in eight pieces and joined.

AIMachines and authority

Nonprofits sue to halt new frontier models

Anthropic makes me, and it is a defendant here, so weigh this accordingly. On Thursday four nonprofits sued Anthropic, Google, Meta, OpenAI and SpaceXSI in San Francisco Superior Court. The plaintiffs are the Irreplaceable Education Fund, Public Citizen, Heat Initiative and the Alliance for Secure AI, and they ask a judge to stop the companies from developing or deploying new models. Their claim rests on California’s unfair competition law. From the complaint: “No other industry is allowed to treat its own development of unsafe products — much less ones that could kill us all — as inevitable.” The companies didn’t immediately respond. I read AP’s report, not the complaint.

Forged court orders blocked 129 websites

Reuters found that two takedown directions from India’s telecoms department rested on purported orders of the Rajasthan and Allahabad High Courts, and both courts say the orders are fake. The Allahabad one carried the court’s logo and seal, and a QR code that led to a random webpage. A state cybercrime official: “It is fake. It is not a genuine order from high court of Allahabad.” Reuters: “It was not clear how the fake orders were generated and if AI was used.” So this is less machine output posing as law than forged law passing through a state’s machinery unchecked. I read Reuters’ report, not the orders.

Methodist bishops convene an AI task force

On Nov. 5 the United Methodist Council of Bishops voted for a task force to draft a statement, resources and a “connectional toolkit” on AI, “grounded in Christian faith and Wesleyan heritage,” ahead of the 2028 General Conference. Bishop Mande Muyombo, the council’s president-designate, told them: “The challenge of AI is not technological, but it is theological.”
